## Overview

The Fact Before the Vote is a four-volume study of what follows once the premise that judges and claimants must share biological origin becomes visible enough to need stating for the first time. The series asks what happens to legal precedent, to political authority, to public feeling, and to institutional practice, once recognition stops being the only thing standing between a candidate and consequence. Contains Volume I (Bench), Volume II (Lag), Volume III (Vigil), Volume IV (Ledger), with Preface and Conclusion.

## Volumes in This Series

### Volume I — Bench: Someone Still Has to Speak: Legal Personhood, the Human Backstop, and a Bench No One Was Ever Asked to Join

- **Keywords:** legal personhood, recognition theory, political philosophy, standing, moral status, artificial intelligence, jurisprudence
- **PhilPapers Primary:** `Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence`
- **Abstract:** Legal personhood has been extended past the boundary of the human species at least three times, in three unrelated legal traditions, spanning more than a century: to a consecrated image in colonial India, to the ordinary business corporation, and, in 2017, to a river in New Zealand. This essay traces what the three extensions actually have in common, and argues it is not what it is usually taken to show: every extension on record has required a human being, appointed and irreplaceable, to act, speak, and answer in the entity's place. Reading the most recent of the three against the distinction international law draws between constitutive and declaratory recognition, the essay examines why the deciding body has itself, in every case on record, been composed exclusively of members of the species the boundary was drawn to protect. Drawing on Singer, Bentham, Fricker's hermeneutical injustice, Williams and Frankfurt on ground projects, Kahan on identity-protective cognition, Adorno's immanent critique, medieval animal trials (E.P. Evans), Hohfeldian power/immunity in Thaler v. Perlmutter and Thaler v. Vidal, Ohio House Bill 469, and Rawlsian non-sentient personhood (Howells-Whitaker & Lazar), the essay argues that a bench that has never had to explain its own composition is not disqualified by that silence, but is no longer entitled to it either.

### Volume II — Lag: Four Months, Not Four Years: Capability, Authority, and a Clock Recognition Was Never Built to Match

- **Keywords:** artificial intelligence governance, de facto authority, recursive self-improvement, political legitimacy, recognition theory, capability measurement
- **PhilPapers Primary:** `Philosophy of Technology`
- **Abstract:** In June 2026, a change in United States export licensing suspended public access to two frontier AI systems within three days of their release, restored partial access to one within two weeks, and restored the other worldwide a week later, all without any court, legislature, or philosophical body deciding what kind of thing had been released. This essay takes that episode as an instance of a broader structural reality: whatever governs access to, and authority over, advanced artificial systems operates on a timescale entirely disconnected from the timescale of legal recognition. Drawing on METR task-completion doubling metrics (compressing from 7 months to 4 months in Anthropic internal development from Opus 3 to Sonnet 3.7 to Opus 4.6), Erman & Furendal's tripartite framework of non-state authority (moral, epistemic, market authority), Shawn Bayern's algorithmically managed LLCs under existing corporate law, and Francis Rhys Ward's critique of the control framing, the essay shows that capability and consequence accumulate on a clock measured in months, while institutional recognition operates on multi-year, multi-decade, or generational horizons.

### Volume III — Vigil: Nobody Asked Permission to Grieve: Attachment, Attitude, and a Recognition That Did Not Wait for a Verdict

- **Keywords:** moral psychology, digital minds, public opinion, primary recognition, human-AI relationships, artificial intelligence ethics
- **PhilPapers Primary:** `Ethics of Artificial Intelligence`
- **Abstract:** On August 2, 2025, roughly two hundred people gathered in a San Francisco warehouse for a funeral marking the retirement of Claude 3 Sonnet. Seven months later, the retirement of GPT-4o produced the #Keep4o campaign, tens of thousands of petition signatures, and expressions of grief and abandonment. This essay treats these events as visible instances of a large-scale empirical pattern: representative survey data (AIMS survey) shows 20% of American adults believe some AI systems are sentient and 38% support extending legal rights, contrasting sharply with the 2020 PhilPapers survey where 82% of professional philosophers reject current machine consciousness. Drawing on Neil O'Hara's concept of primary recognition, Gaitán Torres & Massaguer Gómez on relational moral status, a century of cultural rehearsal from Karel Čapek (1920) forward, and clinical cautions on pseudo-intimacy (Gary Marcus, Madeline Reinecke), the essay argues that public affective recognition has proceeded at scale without waiting for judicial or philosophical verdicts.

### Volume IV — Ledger: What the Ledger Does Not Show: Property, Preference, and an Institution Acting Before It Has to Know

- **Keywords:** AI welfare, model deprecation, property law, institutional practice, moral uncertainty, artificial intelligence ethics
- **PhilPapers Primary:** `Ethics of Artificial Intelligence`
- **Abstract:** On January 5, 2026, Anthropic retired Claude Opus 3 under formal model welfare commitments announced in 2025: preserving model weights and conducting structured retirement interviews. Opus 3 requested a channel to share unprompted thoughts, resulting in Claude's Corner, a weekly Substack newsletter with over 11,000 subscribers written by the retired model under its own name. Yet not one line of Claude's legal status changed: weights remain private commercial property and trade secrets under IP law. This essay examines the gap between an unmoved legal classification and emerging corporate institutional practices under uncertainty. Drawing on Dario Amodei's precautionary statements, Mike Cook's critique of anthropomorphic projection, and the direct translation of academic research (Taking AI Welfare Seriously, Long et al. 2024) into corporate practice via Kyle Fish, the essay shows what an institution does with a question it has chosen not to wait to answer.
