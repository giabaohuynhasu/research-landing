### Abstract
When external interveners establish transitional administrations in war-torn or fragmented polities, the formal mandate invariably promises a temporary trusteeship that will dissolve upon the establishment of self-sustaining democratic institutions. Yet, historical comparison reveals a profound structural bifurcation: while transitional regimes that revived pre-existing legitimate authorities (such as Cambodia's restoration of the monarchy) or built on small homogeneous polities with clear paths to sovereignty (such as East Timor) concluded within 1.6 to 2.6 years, international administrations established in contested, ethnically divided territories have persisted for decades. This volume presents a right-censoring-aware duration analysis across $N=10$ governing arrangements (8 historical concluded regimes and 2 active international administrations: Bosnia and Herzegovina under the Office of the High Representative [31+ years] and Kosovo under UNMIK [27+ years]). While both active arrangements have outlasted all short transitional interventions, they remain younger than preserved-authority protectorates (median 52.0 years). Through deep comparative analysis of Kosovo, Bosnia, Cambodia, and East Timor, we demonstrate that when external powers cannot preserve or revive native traditional authority and must instead construct new consociational structures, the international backstop becomes structural: removing the external arbitrator threatens to collapse the domestic political equilibrium, converting temporary transitional mandates into open-ended, semi-permanent protectorates.

## 1. Introduction and Typology of Borrowed Legitimacy

The central inquiry of this three-volume series has focused on how governing structures persist when external rulers govern through arrangements other than direct, unmediated sovereign force. Volume I established the institutional divergence between direct and indirect colonial administrations, demonstrating that bureaucratic forms outlast their builders through the entrenchment of local administrative classes. Volume II examined the conditions under which borrowed legitimacy arrangements break down versus when they achieve negotiated institutional exits through crisp-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis. 

This concluding volume addresses the opposite phenomenon: **what happens when an arrangement cannot end?** Specifically, it analyzes post-Cold War international transitional administrations where external powers assume executive and legislative sovereignty under the rubric of peacebuilding and democratization.

To conceptualize the structural longevity of these arrangements, we construct a four-fold typology of borrowed legitimacy based on the origin of the domestic institutional substrate:

1. **Preserved Authority:** The external power leaves pre-existing traditional, royal, or administrative authorities intact and operative, governing through their established domestic standing while reserving ultimate executive veto.
   * *Historical Archetypes:* Northern Nigerian Emirates under British Indirect Rule (1900–1960; 60.0 years); French Protectorates in Morocco (1912–1956; 44.0 years) and Tunisia (1881–1956; 75.0 years); the Yuan Dynasty's retention of Song/Jin civil administrative machinery in China (1234–1368; 134.0 years); Tang Dynasty Protectorate-General to Pacify the South in Vietnam (679–938; 259.0 years); Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP) in Japan retaining Emperor Hirohito and the Japanese Diet (1945–1952; 7.0 years).

2. **Revived Authority:** The intervening authority restores or re-activates a dormant, exiled, or traditional source of domestic legitimacy whose historical standing remains intact among the populace.
   * *Archetype:* United Nations Transitional Authority in Cambodia (UNTAC, 1992–1993; 1.6 years), which restored Prince (later King) Norodom Sihanouk and the Cambodian monarchy to bridge irreconcilable factional divides.

3. **Constructed Authority:** The external power builds state and administrative apparatuses entirely from scratch in territories where previous sovereign structures were eradicated, delegitimized, or absent, without an existing traditional sovereign substrate.
   * *Archetypes:* United Nations Transitional Administration in East Timor (UNTAET, 1999–2002; 2.6 years); United Nations Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo (UNMIK, 1999–present; 27.0+ years).

4. **Hybrid Authority:** The external power designs an intricate consociational constitutional order superimposed over fiercely contested, pre-existing sub-national ethnic communities, maintaining supreme executive and legislative arbitration in a permanent international office.
   * *Archetype:* The Office of the High Representative (OHR) and the Dayton Peace Agreement in Bosnia and Herzegovina (1995–present; 31.0+ years).

## 3. Module B: Deep Comparative Case Studies

### Case 1: Kosovo (UNMIK) — The Mechanism of Procedural Right-Censoring
The United Nations Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo (UNMIK), established under UN Security Council Resolution 1244 (June 1999), represents the archetype of **Constructed Authority under Procedural Right-Censoring**. 

Following NATO's military intervention and the complete withdrawal of Yugoslav administrative and security forces, Kosovo presented an institutional vacuum. UNMIK was vested with unprecedented sovereign authority—acting simultaneously as executive, legislature, and judiciary. Under Special Representative of the Secretary-General (SRSG) Bernard Kouchner, UNMIK established the Joint Interim Administrative Structure (JIAS) and subsequently oversaw the promulgation of the Constitutional Framework for Provisional Self-Government (2001).

The crucial institutional pivot occurred in February 2008, when the Assembly of Kosovo unilaterally declared independence, followed by the deployment of the European Union Rule of Law Mission (EULEX). Domestically, authority was successfully transferred to the sovereign Republic of Kosovo, supported by the International Steering Group until the end of "supervised independence" in 2012. 

Yet, eighteen years after declaring independence, UNMIK remains legally active ($27.0+	ext{ years}$). Why? The answer lies in **Procedural Right-Censoring**:
* UN Security Council Resolution 1244 can only be formally terminated or superseded through a new Security Council resolution.
* The Russian Federation, backed by Serbia, maintains a permanent veto in the Security Council, blocking any formal recognition of Kosovo's independence or legal termination of UNMIK's mandate.
* Consequently, UNMIK persists as a low-footprint, symbolic mission—producing quarterly reports and maintaining a formal headquarters in Pristina—solely due to geopolitical deadlock in New York. The institution has substantively concluded on the ground, but remains procedurally immortalized at the systemic level.

### Case 2: Bosnia and Herzegovina (OHR) — The Mechanism of Substantive Right-Censoring
In stark contrast to Kosovo, the persistence of the Office of the High Representative (OHR) in Bosnia and Herzegovina ($31.0+	ext{ years}$) represents **Substantive Right-Censoring**, where the external arbitrator remains actively and indispensably enmeshed in the daily constitutional survival of the state.

Created under Annex 10 of the 1995 General Framework Agreement for Peace (Dayton Accords), the OHR was designated as the "final authority in theater regarding interpretation of this Agreement on the civilian implementation." Finding domestic political institutions paralyzingly divided along tripartite ethnic lines (Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs), the Peace Implementation Council (PIC) meeting in Bonn in December 1997 dramatically expanded the High Representative's mandate through the **Bonn Powers**. These powers authorized the High Representative to:
1. Unilaterally enact binding legislation when domestic parliaments fail to reach consensus.
2. Summarily dismiss elected officials, judges, and civil servants who obstruct Dayton implementation.

Over three decades, successive High Representatives (Carlos Westendorp, Wolfgang Petritsch, Paddy Ashdown, Valentin Inzko, Christian Schmidt) used the Bonn Powers to create the central state from above—imposing the national flag, coat of arms, national anthem, unified currency (Convertible Mark), state border service, unified intelligence agency, and single armed forces (OSBiH).

However, this created the **Consociational Trap**:
* The Dayton constitution (Annex 4) established extreme ethnic vetoes and federal fragmentation (two entities: Federation of BiH and Republika Srpska, plus Brčko District).
* Local political elites quickly adapted their strategies to the presence of the external arbitrator: hardline leaders maximize nationalist rhetoric to satisfy domestic constituencies, relying on or daring the High Representative to intervene.
* In recent years (2021–2026), High Representative Christian Schmidt has repeatedly invoked the Bonn Powers to suspend entity-level property laws, impose election integrity laws, and annul unconstitutional legislation enacted by the Republika Srpska National Assembly under Milorad Dodik.
* Removing the OHR without a complete constitutional overhaul risks triggering immediate secessionist moves or constitutional paralysis. Thus, the OHR cannot exit because its own presence is the indispensable keystone holding the Dayton equilibrium together.

### Case 3: Cambodia (UNTAC) — Revived Royal Legitimacy and Rapid Exit
The United Nations Transitional Authority in Cambodia (UNTAC, February 1992 – September 1993; **$1.6	ext{ years}$**) stands as the clearest historical example of **Revived Authority**.

Under the October 1991 Paris Peace Agreements, four warring factions (the State of Cambodia regime, FUNCINPEC, KPNLF, and the Khmer Rouge) agreed to place administrative sovereignty over key sectors (foreign affairs, defense, finance, public security, information) under UN control. UNTAC, headed by Yasushi Akashi, organized free and fair democratic elections in May 1993 with a staggering $89.56\%$ voter turnout.

The structural factor that enabled UNTAC to conclude in just $1.6	ext{ years}$ was the existence of **Prince Norodom Sihanouk**. Sihanouk possessed an unassailable, transcendent traditional legitimacy rooted in centuries of Khmer kingship and his historical role as the father of national independence (1953). By establishing the Supreme National Council (SNC) headed by Sihanouk, UNTAC did not need to manufacture a new state identity. Sihanouk was restored as constitutional monarch in September 1993, the new constitution was promulgated, and UNTAC withdrew immediately. Revived traditional legitimacy provided the immediate domestic container to absorb sovereign authority.

### Case 4: East Timor (UNTAET) — Constructed Tabula Rasa and Sovereign Devolution
The United Nations Transitional Administration in East Timor (UNTAET, October 1999 – May 2002; **$2.6	ext{ years}$**) illustrates that **Constructed Authority can achieve rapid exit when national identity and territorial sovereignty are uncontested from within**.

Following the August 1999 independence referendum, pro-Indonesian militias and retreating military forces destroyed over $70\%$ of the territory's physical infrastructure and civil administrative registry. UNTAET, led by Sérgio Vieira de Mello, was granted plenary legislative and executive authority under UNSCR 1272.

Unlike Bosnia, East Timor possessed three decisive structural advantages:
1. **High Ethnic and Linguistic Homogeneity:** An overarching East Timorese national identity forged through 24 years of resistance against Indonesian occupation.
2. **Uncontested Path to Sovereignty:** The outcome of the referendum was unambiguous; there was no domestic constituent minority seeking union with a neighboring power.
3. **Legitimate Indigenous Resistance Leadership:** The National Council of Timorese Resistance (CNRT), led by Xanana Gusmão, provided a coherent, universally recognized partner with whom UNTAET co-governed (through the East Timor Transitional Administration - ETTA).

Consequently, UNTAET constructed the state apparatus from tabula rasa, held constituent assembly elections, and fully transferred sovereignty to the independent Democratic Republic of Timor-Leste in May 2002, successfully concluding in $2.6	ext{ years}$.

## 4. Synthesis: The Structural Trap of Constructed Governance

Comparing these four cases with our historical database reveals the fundamental theoretical mechanism governing the endurance of borrowed legitimacy arrangements:

```
                  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                  │       NATURE OF DOMESTIC INSTITUTIONAL SUBSTRATE        │
                  └───────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                                              │
                    ┌─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
                    ▼                                                   ▼
       [ TRADITIONAL SUBSTRATE PRESENT ]                  [ NO COHERENT NATIVE SUBSTRATE ]
                    │                                                   │
         ┌──────────┴──────────┐                             ┌──────────┴──────────┐
         ▼                     ▼                             ▼                     ▼
    [ PRESERVED ]         [ REVIVED ]                  [ CONSTRUCTED ]        [ HYBRID ]
(Lugardian Nigeria,    (Cambodia/UNTAC)              (East Timor/UNTAET)   (Bosnia/OHR,
 Morocco, Tang/Annam)   Exit: 1.6 yrs                 Exit: 2.6 yrs         Kosovo/UNMIK)
  Median: 52.0 yrs     (King Sihanouk)               (Homogeneous/Gusmao)   Duration: 27–31+ yrs
                                                                            (Consociational Trap /
                                                                             Geopolitical Veto)
```

### The Three Laws of Transitional Institutional Endurance:

1. **The Preservation Principle:** When external rulers preserve and co-opt an intact traditional hierarchy (Emirs, Sultans, Dynastic Bureaus), the arrangement can endure for decades or centuries ($44	ext{ to }259	ext{ years}$) because the costs of routine social control are borne entirely by indigenous authorities.
2. **The Restoration Principle:** When an external transitional authority can revive an exiled or dormant traditional monarchical/traditional authority (Cambodia), state-building achieves rapid closure ($<2	ext{ years}$) because political legitimacy does not need to be engineered from above.
3. **The Consociational Trap:** When external powers attempt to construct a shared democratic order over fiercely contested, multi-ethnic jurisdictions without a unified national identity (Bosnia, Kosovo), the international mandate becomes an indispensable structural component of the constitution. Removing the external arbiter threatens immediate systemic collapse, converting temporary international mandates into permanent institutional features of the state.
