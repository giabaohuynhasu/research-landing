ABOUT THIS SERIES
A research programme develops its own working methods long before anyone states them as a 
method. Between 2026 and the present, an independent research programme on longevity, institutions, 
and artificial intelligence produced its results by a specific practice: writing from inside the events under 
analysis, in sustained collaboration with a large language model, while withholding prescriptive  
conclusions and stating in advance what would count as evidence against its own claims.
War Correspondent Philosophy names, tests, and states the falsification conditions for that  
practice. Across six volumes, the series treats the practice itself as an object of study: what proximity to 
an unfolding event can and cannot license, what a researcher owes a claim before treating it as safe from 
revision, why a sequence of dated publications can carry evidential weight a single publication cannot, 
what changes when part of the cognitive labor is performed by an instrument with no memory of its own 
prior contributions, when a formal mechanism identified in one domain is genuinely present in another 
rather than merely resembling it, and what a framework is obligated to do with itself once part of it has 
been shown false.
This series does not defend the substantive conclusions of the research programme it examines. 
It asks whether the programme followed the method it claims to follow, and specifies the terms on which 
that claim could be shown wrong.
The question running through all six volumes:
What obligations bind a researcher who writes from inside the events a research 
programme analyzes, in collaboration with an instrument that is itself part of the 
event?

ANALYTICAL ESSAY
Mechanism Transfer and Domain Portability: When a Formal 
Structure Survives Its Crossing Into a New Domain
Gia Bao Huynh*
Independent Researcher, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam
Two things that look alike can look alike for at least three different reasons: shared  
origin, independent arrival at the same form, or superficial resemblance that dissolves under  
scrutiny. A research programme that repeatedly moves a formal mechanism from one domain  
into an unrelated one needs a way to distinguish these cases, or every cross-domain claim it  
makes is vulnerable to the objection that the resemblance was rhetorical rather than structural. 
This essay describes a demanding standard for a genuine transfer, developed and applied to a  
case in which an evolutionary mechanism is proposed to operate literally, rather than by  
analogy, in the history of a regional institution. The standard requires a domain to literally  
satisfy the formal preconditions of the mechanism in question — a population of entities, a  
process that transmits structure between generations with reasonable fidelity, and variation that 
causes some entities to persist and others not to — rather than merely resembling a domain  
where the mechanism is already accepted. It requires identifying with precision which entity  
plays which structural role, since a transferred mechanism does not automatically come with its 
components pre-labeled. And it prefers a specific, counterintuitive kind of evidence: two  
systems that arrive at the same structure with no shared history between them provide stronger 
confirmation that the structure is doing real explanatory work than two systems that share the  
structure because they share an ancestor, since independent origin rules out inheritance as the  
explanation in a way shared descent cannot — though, as the essay's closing sections make  
clear, the biological version of this test is considerably cleaner than any institutional application 
of it can claim to be. Applying the standard to a specific historical case shows it capable of  
explaining a data pattern that a cruder transfer could only redescribe, by revealing two  
genuinely distinct underlying strategies where a flatter comparison saw only inconsistent  
measurement. The same rigor, applied honestly, can also show a historically successful strategy 
falling short by orders of magnitude of what a harder version of the same problem would  
require — a sober finding a careless transfer would be more likely to paper over than to state.

4 Mechanism Transfer and Domain Portability
*Corresponding author. Email: huynhbao@asu.edu. ORCID: 0009-0008-2372-5852.
1  Introduction
Two things that look alike can look alike for at least three different reasons, and telling 
them apart matters enormously for what the resemblance is allowed to prove. They might share 
a common origin, so the resemblance is inherited rather than independently arrived at. They  
might have reached the same form independently, for reasons that have nothing to do with  
shared history, in which case the resemblance is evidence that something about the form itself is 
doing real work. Or they might simply resemble each other in a handful of surface respects  
while differing in everything that actually matters, in which case the resemblance proves  
nothing and the comparison was never more than a metaphor. A research programme that  
repeatedly moves a formal mechanism identified in one domain into an entirely different one — 
biology into institutional history, in the case this volume examines — needs a way to tell these 
three cases apart, or every cross-domain claim it makes is vulnerable to the same objection: that 
the mechanism does not actually transfer, and the resemblance was rhetorical.
2  A Claim About Domain-Independence
Geoffrey  Hodgson  and  Thorbjørn  Knudsen’s  account  of  generalized  Darwinism 
supplies a demanding standard for what would make a transfer more than rhetorical. They argue 
that variation, a mechanism of inheritance that transmits traits between generations with  
reasonable fidelity, and selection produced by that variation causing differential persistence are 
jointly necessary and sufficient conditions for a Darwinian explanation, and that these  
conditions are domain-independent: wherever a population of entities literally varies, some  
mechanism literally transmits structure between generations, and that variation literally causes 
some entities to persist and others not to, a Darwinian explanation literally applies — not as a  
suggestive analogy borrowed from biology, but as the same explanatory structure biology  
happens to be where the pattern was first described (Hodgson & Knudsen, 2010). The claim is 
demanding because it is falsifiable in a specific way: a domain either does or does not contain a 
population, a fidelity-preserving transmission mechanism, and variation-driven differential  
persistence, and if it does not, invoking evolutionary language about that domain is exactly the 
empty metaphor the standard exists to rule out.
3  Two Ways to Preserve a Structure
Testing whether a domain literally satisfies the standard requires knowing precisely  
what is doing the transmitting and what is doing the interacting with the environment, a  
distinction the biologist and philosopher David Hull formalized as the difference between a  
replicator, the unit that passes on structure with reasonable fidelity, and an interactor, the unit  
that engages the environment as a cohesive whole and whose success or failure in that  
engagement makes replication differential (Hull, 1988). In the biological case these are usually 
easy to separate: genes replicate, organisms interact with the world and live or die. In an  
institutional  case  the  separation  takes  real  analytical  work.  A  template  —  a  specific  
configuration of frontier defense doctrine, fiscal administration, and a protocol for absorbing  
displaced elites, say — can function as a replicator: a structure capable of being reconstructed 
with reasonable fidelity long after the specific regime that last used it has disappeared, dormant 
for centuries with no interactor expressing it at all, and re-expressed as a new interactor — a

new regime, built by different people, in a different generation — whenever the environmental 
condition that originally selected for the template recurs. Establishing that a replicator persisted 
through a genuine gap in interactor expression, and was reconstructed rather than merely  
resembled by coincidence when the environment recurred, is what separates a literal claim  
about institutional inheritance from a loose metaphor about history repeating itself.
4  The Test That Actually Discriminates
Even a genuine replicator-interactor relationship leaves an ambiguity a careful transfer 
has to resolve: entities that share a profile of traits might share it because they share a common 
replicator, which biologists call homology, or because they arrived at the same profile  
independently, from different starting points, with no shared history at all, which they call  
convergence. This distinction supplies a more discriminating test than resemblance alone, and 
in a direction that is not obvious in advance: convergence is the stronger evidence, not the  
weaker one. Two interactors that share a profile because they share a replicator have not  
independently confirmed that the profile does real adaptive work; they have inherited the same 
structure, and their agreement is one data point wearing the appearance of two. Two interactors 
with no shared history at all, arriving at the same profile from entirely separate starting  
conditions, cannot be explained by inheritance, which leaves the shared environmental  
pressure, and whatever the profile does in response to it, as the only remaining explanation  
available. A set of institutions descended from one common template, compared against one  
another, tests whether the template was faithfully copied. A set of institutions with no common 
ancestor  at  all,  compared  against  one  another,  tests  whether  a  particular  structure  is  
independently adaptive under a particular kind of pressure — a stronger and more informative 
test, precisely because independent origin removes the explanation a shared ancestor would  
otherwise supply for free.
The standard is easier to apply in biology than the transfer to institutions might suggest, 
and the difference is worth naming rather than passing over. Common ancestry in the biological 
case is a comparatively clean, checkable condition, because genetic inheritance is close to the  
only channel through which trait information moves between lineages; two lineages either  
share a genetic ancestor or they do not, and the question is normally decidable in principle even 
when it is hard to decide in practice. Institutions have no equivalent clean channel. A template 
can travel between two states that share no political lineage at all through trade, conquest,  
translated administrative texts, or diplomatic observation, producing a real category the  
biological binary has no slot for: partial, documented cultural influence well short of the direct 
institutional  inheritance  a  true  replicator  relationship  requires,  but  well  short  of  true  
independence too. Ruling out this middle case is a harder empirical burden in an institutional  
application than ruling out shared ancestry ordinarily is in a biological one, and treating the two 
as equally clean would overstate what the institutional version of the test can deliver.
5  Same Mechanism, Different Strategies
A further precision keeps a genuine mechanism transfer from collapsing two distinct  
patterns into one. Ecological theory distinguishes K-selected strategies, which invest heavily in 
a small number of long-lived individuals optimized to endure, from r-selected strategies, which 
favor rapid turnover and recolonization, with persistence maintained at the level of the  
population rather than any single long-lived member of it (MacArthur & Wilson, 1967).  
Applied to institutions facing a recurring disruption, this distinction explains rather than merely 
redescribes  a  pattern  that  would  otherwise  look  like  measurement  inconsistency:  two  
institutions can respond to a structurally similar threat with genuinely different, sometimes

competing strategies — one investing enormous resources into a single enduring interactor, the 
other tolerating the repeated extinction of individual interactors so long as the replicator  
survives to be re-expressed — and a measurement that ranks them inconsistently across  
different metrics may not be measuring the same thing badly. It may be correctly detecting two 
different quantities. Richard Levins’s condition for the persistence of a population distributed  
across a shifting patchwork of local sites — that the rate of recolonizing empty sites exceed the 
rate at which occupied sites go locally extinct — gives the r-selected strategy a precise,  
checkable requirement of its own (Levins, 1969), distinct from whatever requirement governs 
the K-selected alternative, rather than a vaguer sense that persistence is somehow good.
6  What a Genuine Transfer Still Doesn’t Give You
Confirming that a mechanism genuinely transfers is not the same as confirming that a  
historically successful response transfers with it, and the distinction matters most exactly where 
it is most tempting to blur. A domain can satisfy every formal condition for a given  
evolutionary strategy, and historical instances of that strategy can be real, literal, non-
metaphorical successes, without those successes being large enough in magnitude to meet a  
substantially  larger  version  of  the  same  challenge.  Measuring  a  historically  realized  
recolonization rate against the rate a new and more demanding disruption regime would require 
can show a strategy working exactly as the theory predicts while still falling short by orders of 
magnitude of what a harder problem would now need — a finding a mechanism transfer is  
positioned to state with some precision, and a more useful thing for it to establish than a  
reassuring but unearned conclusion that history has already solved the harder problem too.
7  What This Doesn’t Settle
This volume has described what distinguishes a mechanism transfer that is genuine  
from one that is merely evocative: literal satisfaction of a domain-independent standard, a  
precise account of what plays the replicator role and what plays the interactor role, a preference 
for convergent over inherited evidence tempered by an honest accounting of how much harder 
that preference is to apply outside biology, and enough strategic precision to avoid mistaking  
two different quantities for one badly measured quantity. It has not addressed what a framework 
built this carefully is obligated to do with itself once one of its own components is shown to  
have failed — including a component, like the strategic shortfall this volume’s own closing  
section identifies, that a transfer this rigorous can reveal to be insufficient rather than  
successful. That is the subject of the volume that closes this series.
