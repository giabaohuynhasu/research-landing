Miranda v. Arizona: You Have the Right to Remain Silent
Initial Post — 208 words

In Miranda v. Arizona (1966), the Supreme Court considered whether statements obtained during custodial police interrogation could be admitted when suspects had not been informed of their constitutional rights. Relying on the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, the Court held that custodial interrogation is inherently coercive and therefore requires affirmative procedural warnings—including the rights to remain silent and to have counsel—before statements may be admissible.

Comment 1 — Psychological Coercion: Chief Justice Warren’s discussion of incommunicado interrogation demonstrates that coercion does not require physical violence; isolation, authority, uncertainty, and sustained questioning can create powerful psychological pressure. Miranda therefore recognizes how interrogation conditions can undermine a suspect’s ability to make a genuinely voluntary decision.

Comment 2 — Constitutional Linkage: Miranda builds on Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) and Escobedo v. Illinois (1964), which strengthened the constitutional role of counsel. Together, these cases reflect an expanding principle that constitutional rights must be meaningful during critical interactions with the criminal justice system.

Comment 3 — Institutional Trade-off: Miranda protections can make investigations more procedurally demanding, but that cost reflects a deliberate trade-off between crime-control efficiency and due process.

Comment 4 — Discussion Question: Should police receive additional safeguards or obligations when questioning juveniles or non-native English speakers, whose vulnerability may make standard Miranda warnings less effective?
